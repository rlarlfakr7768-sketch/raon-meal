Why microwaves leave hot and cold spots

Food on the same plate can heat unevenly inside a microwave.
The electric field varies across the oven, changing how much energy different regions absorb.
Waves reflecting from metal walls overlap, forming patterns called standing waves.
Their nodes and antinodes stay fixed as the field oscillates.
At the usual frequency, microwaves have a wavelength around twelve centimeters in air.
In a one-dimensional standing wave, neighboring antinodes are roughly six centimeters apart.
For unchanged material properties and frequency, absorbed power per volume scales with field amplitude squared.
Doubling that amplitude therefore gives four times the average absorbed power.
Rotation carries each part of the food through different electric field strengths.
This can reduce differences in the energy those parts absorb over time.
The center stays put, while food shape and composition also alter the field.
Rotation alone doesn't guarantee even temperatures throughout the food on the plate.
